AI infrastructure

We build the thing. Not a slide deck about the thing.

Most firms selling AI advice will write you a policy and a roadmap and stop at the point where somebody has to make hardware work. We start there. One private inference box in your rack, or ten thousand GPUs with a fabric that has to actually carry the traffic. Same discipline either way. Which layer fails first changes with scale, and that is the whole job. At a few nodes it is the scheduler. At a few hundred it is the fabric — routing, congestion, and whether RDMA is carrying the traffic or has silently fallen back to TCP. Past that it is storage bandwidth, then power and cooling. A vendor benchmark will not tell you which one you have, partly because the storage array's own write buffer can defeat the test you were told to run.

What we look for

You have probably said one of these out loud.

Every operation loses money in three ways at once, and most reviews are commissioned to look at one of them. What each one is called and where it hides changes by sector, which is why there is a page for your industry rather than one page asking you to translate.

01 — LEAKAGE

“Revenue is up. Somehow the cash isn’t.”

  • Billing, collection, AR/AP and write-offs
  • Vendor overpayment and duplicate services
  • Contract terms nobody enforces
  • Rates and discounts never trued up
RECOVERABLE Cash you go and get back. Usually the first thing to move, and it is what pays for the review.
02 — DRAG

“We are busier than ever and it isn’t reaching the bottom line.”

  • Reconciliation, rekeying and approval queues
  • Rework loops, and what causes them upstream
  • Time from hire to productive, and what it waits on
  • Handoffs between sales, delivery and finance
REPEATING Capacity you stop buying, or start selling. Moves the run rate rather than the quarter.
03 — EXPOSURE

“The audit found something nobody knew was there.”

  • Compliance and regulatory stance
  • Legal and contractual exposure
  • Unmanaged AI or data sharing
  • Key-person and uninsured liability
CONTINGENT Loss you avoid. Worth nothing until the day it is worth everything.

What the review finds is what the review finds. Nothing above is a result we are claiming; it is where we look. We write down what we expect to find before fieldwork, so afterwards you can check us against it rather than take our word for what was always going to be in there.

Before you commission it

You see everything first. Nothing moves without you.

Commissioning a proper look takes a little nerve, because whatever it turns up concerns people you hired and trusted. So: findings go to you and to nobody else, anything uncomfortable is a spoken conversation before it is written down anywhere, and we read systems rather than people. Almost none of what we find is anyone’s fault — concentrated loss is what busy, well-run operations produce. Exactly what happens to a finding, and the terms we work to.

Method

We review the operating system, not one department.

Loss concentrates; it does not distribute. One contract, one queue, one account nobody reconciles. Thirty sampled transactions are the wrong instrument for finding something that only happened once — and a finance-only review never opens the contract file at all.

CONVENTIONAL

  • Samples thirty transactions
  • Interviews eight people
  • Reviews one quarter
  • Extrapolates the rest

Regulated environments

The rules the engagement has to live inside.

A review of the whole operation reaches the parts of it that are regulated. Payroll, contracts, the general ledger, customer records and in some sectors patient or student records all sit inside somebody’s regime, and the review does not get an exemption from it. PHI under a signed BAA and the HIPAA Security Rule. CUI to NIST SP 800-171, with the SSP and POA&M that go with it. EAR and ITAR where they apply — including the part that catches research organizations, where a document request is a form of access. GDPR, FERPA, and PCI DSS handled by keeping cardholder data out of scope entirely.

Capability and commitment, not certification. There is no such thing as being “HIPAA certified”, and a firm that says otherwise has told you something useful about the rest of its claims. What we may hold, where, who may see it and how it is destroyed is agreed in writing before the first document moves — the whole of it is written down here, and a security questionnaire can be completed before you commission anything.
