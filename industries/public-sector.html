Public sector

Statutory duty, and the cost of discharging it.

Public bodies cannot choose not to deliver. What they can choose is how, and the how is rarely examined across services — each one optimizing locally while the shared cost sits between them.

Where it concentrates here

Five losses that recur in the public sector and are worth going looking for. Each is concentrated — a single agreement, a single queue, a single account nobody reviews — which is precisely why a sample of the operation tends to miss them and a full pass tends to find them. They are not findings from a named engagement; they are what a review of the public sector is built to look for.

  1. Unit cost of a service varying substantially between neighboring areas with similar demand, unexamined because the comparison is nobody's job.
  2. Fees and charges set historically and never uprated to cost recovery, subsidising users at the expense of the general fund.
  3. Direct awards and off-framework procurement accumulating without aggregate visibility.
  4. Property portfolio holding cost carried by services that do not see it and cannot act on it.
  5. Demand management effort concentrated at the point of crisis rather than upstream, where it is cheaper.

Not every the public sector is the same business

  • CountiesMedicaid local share, social services and the correctional facility consuming the levy-funded side of the budget, with placement and contract rates set by the state and by a provider market the county does not control.
  • Cities, towns and villagesA tax levy small enough that one contract — sanitation, ambulance, a shared highway agreement — is a material share of it.
  • School districts and BOCESA budget the voters approve, a state aid claim that drives the revenue side, and a cost base fixed by contracts that keep running on their own terms after they expire.
  • Public authorities, IDAs and special districtsWater, sewer, fire, library and industrial development boards with their own budgets, their own boards and their own reporting, sitting outside the host municipality's normal monitoring.
  • Police, fire and EMSHeadcount-driven cost with overtime as the only flex, minimum staffing written into the contract, and mutual aid agreements whose cost shares were agreed once.
  • Component units and shared servicesA local development corporation, an authority or a shared service billed to the parent on a basis nobody has re-costed since it was set up.

Where AI is already worth the money here

  • The monthly budget-to-actual narrativeEvery month the budget office exports the ledger, rebuilds the same variance report, and writes the same explanations department by department. A model reading that export against the prior three months drafts the first pass — which line moved, by how much, and whether it moved last month too. The analyst corrects it and signs it. What goes away is the typing, not the judgment.
  • One vendor, spelled four waysThe vendor master holds the same company under four spellings, and purchasing, the attorney and the department each keep their own contract file. Matching those into a single cumulative spend per vendor is name matching, which a model does reliably, and it produces a number no single file holds: everything bought from one company this year, a purchase order at a time.
  • Grant closeout before the clawbackSLFRF money has to be spent by December 31, 2026, and proving it was spent means tying every charge to an approved budget line and an allowable cost category under 2 CFR 200. Reading the award terms and the general ledger detail together and flagging what does not tie is weeks of a grants accountant's time now. It is also the exact work the single audit will do later, at your expense.
  • The fee schedule against what the service costsFees are set in local laws and resolutions adopted years apart, and nobody holds the whole list in one place. Pulling every fee out of the code and the adopted schedules into one table is extraction work — slow by hand, quick with a model — and it is the step that has to happen before anyone can set a fee against the labor minutes behind it.
  • Records requestsFOIL requests arrive continuously and the search is done by whoever has time that week. A model can assemble the responsive set from the mail store and the shared drives and propose an exemption for each withholding. The records access officer still decides every one and still signs the response — but on a stack that arrives sorted.
  • Overtime against what the contract actually saysCall-out order, minimum staffing, what triggers premium pay: it is all in the collective bargaining agreement, and payroll applies it from memory. A model reading the agreement alongside the time records flags the shifts where the rule and the payment disagree. Payroll still verifies each flag — but flagging is the part nobody is staffed to do.

None of this needs a model smarter than your budget director. It needs one that will read all of it, every month, which is the part nobody has the headcount for.

Where this data actually lives

  • Tyler Enterprise ERP — still called Munis by everyone who uses it — as the ledger; CentralSquare Finance Enterprise or Naviline in mid-size agencies, Springbrook in villages and districts, Williamson Law Book in the smallest towns
  • For counties, the state systems the caseworkers actually live in — CONNECTIONS for child welfare, the state eligibility system for public assistance and Medicaid — which report to Albany, not to your ledger
  • Tax and assessment in their own stack — RPS and Image Mate for the roll (Systems Development Group was bought by Schneider Geospatial in 2024) — with collection sitting in the receiver's or treasurer's office and not posting to the ledger in real time
  • Accela, Tyler EnerGov or Municity (now sold by ICC Community Development Solutions) for permits, codes and licensing — where the fee schedule lives, and where the cost of issuing the permit does not
  • A budget worksheet per department that is the real working document between the ledger and the adopted budget, rebuilt from scratch every year by one person
  • Contract files in three places — purchasing, the attorney and the department — that do not agree with each other, and a vendor master with the same vendor entered four ways

Named because it changes what the intake asks for. An extract from one of these has known gaps, and the review is built around them rather than surprised by them.

The obligations that set the deadlines

  • The annual comprehensive financial report — the ACFR, renamed from CAFR in 2021 — under GASB, and the audit opinion under Government Auditing Standards
  • The annual financial report to the State Comptroller under General Municipal Law §30 — the AUD — due 60 days after the close of the fiscal year, 120 days for counties, cities, and towns and villages over 20,000
  • The tax cap calculation filed with the State Comptroller before the budget is adopted, and the local law it takes a 60 percent vote of the board to override
  • Grant awards and their conditions, including disallowance and recovery of ineligible costs, and the single audit that tests them once federal spending crosses $1,000,000 in a year — raised from $750,000 for fiscal years beginning on or after October 1, 2024
  • The NYSLRS employer invoice, issued in November and payable February 1, with a discount for prepaying by December 15 — a cash decision made once a year and rarely modeled
  • Competitive bidding thresholds under General Municipal Law §103 — over $20,000 for purchase contracts, over $35,000 for public works — and the procurement policy §104-b requires the board to adopt and review every year
  • The SLFRF expenditure deadline of December 31, 2026 — funds had to be obligated by December 31, 2024, and anything unspent when the clock runs out goes back to Treasury

When this stops being interesting and becomes urgent

  • A projected deficit in the monthly budget-to-actual that grows at each report
  • A material weakness, or a repeat finding in the schedule of findings and questioned costs
  • Fund balance drawn down two years running to hold the levy under the cap
  • An out-of-district special education or foster care placement rate moving faster than the budget that funds it
  • An ERP or case management replacement, which surfaces every local workaround
  • A shared services or consolidation study, where an intermunicipal charge is examined for the first time since it was set
  • A fiscal stress score from the State Comptroller, or an OSC audit and the corrective action plan that follows it
  • A collective bargaining agreement running past its expiry, where the steps keep moving under Triborough while nothing is signed

Who this involves

  • Signs it: The comptroller, finance commissioner or director of finance, with the county executive, mayor, supervisor or superintendent
  • Resists it: Department heads and commissioners, whose cost per unit becomes comparable with the next county over
  • Has been asking for it: The budget office, which has been writing the monthly variance reports nobody acts on
