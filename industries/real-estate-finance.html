Real estate finance

What the operating agreement says, and what the model computes.

The buildings are one business and the capital structure is another, and the second is where the money is actually decided. Waterfalls, capital accounts and covenant definitions are agreed once, then computed for years by a spreadsheet nobody else can read. A review reconciles the computation to the document.

Where it concentrates here

7 losses that recur in a real estate sponsor and are worth going looking for. Each is concentrated — a single agreement, a single queue, a single account nobody reviews — which is precisely why a sample of the operation tends to miss them and a full pass tends to find them. They are not findings from a named engagement; they are what a review of a real estate sponsor is built to look for.

  1. A distribution waterfall computed by a spreadsheet that has never been reconciled, clause by clause, to the operating agreement it implements.
  2. Preferred return accruing at a rate and compounding on a basis that the model and the agreement describe differently, discovered at the crystallisation event.
  3. Covenant ratios reported against a definition borrowed from another loan — DSCR and debt yield are defined per document, and the definitions differ.
  4. Affiliated fees — asset management, construction management, acquisition — charged to deals at rates that were never re-consented as the platform grew.
  5. Investor capital accounts maintained in the fund system and in the tax return with no reconciliation between them.
  6. Development budgets reforecast informally while the lender still holds the original, making a routine overrun a covenant event nobody flagged.
  7. Promote already distributed against a clawback that has never been modelled under a downside case.

Not every real estate sponsor is the same business

  • Sponsors and syndicatorsDeal-by-deal entities, each with its own waterfall, promote and capital accounts, and no consolidated view of what the sponsor actually earned.
  • Funds and programmatic capitalPreferred return accruals and clawback exposure that is computed once a year, usually by the person who built the model.
  • Property management armsManagement and construction management fees earned from affiliated entities, which is exactly where a related-party question lands.
  • DevelopmentCapitalised interest, draw schedules and cost-to-complete, where a budget reforecast is a covenant event.
  • Affordable and tax-credit housingLIHTC compliance periods, investor reporting and recapture exposure that survives long after the deal closes.

Where this data actually lives

  • Yardi Voyager or MRI at the property level; Investran or Juniper Square for the fund and investor layer
  • Excel for the waterfall — almost always, and almost always unreconciled to the operating agreement
  • AppFolio or Entrata in the smaller property management operations
  • A separate lender reporting pack per loan, assembled by hand each quarter

Named because it changes what the intake asks for. An extract from one of these has known gaps, and the review is built around them rather than surprised by them.

The obligations that set the deadlines

  • Lender covenant certificates — DSCR, debt yield, LTV — and the calculation definitions that differ per loan document
  • Investor capital account statements and K-1s, tied to the operating agreement
  • SEC Regulation D filings and, where relevant, Form D amendments
  • LIHTC and HUD compliance reporting where applicable
  • Property tax appeals and the deadlines that cannot be extended

What the review would ask you for

9 requests written for a real estate sponsor and asked of nobody else — 6 of them core. They sit alongside the operating spine every business has — finance, revenue, receivables, payables, contracts, people, IT, operations, risk, commercial and governance — which is 107 further requests.
  • Entity map: sponsors, funds, JVs and property SPEs Core Ownership percentages and who controls each. This is usually the first thing nobody has current.
  • Operating agreements and the distribution waterfall for each deal Core The agreement itself, alongside the model that computes it.
  • Investor capital accounts and contribution/distribution history Core Per investor per entity, reconciled to the agreement.
  • Loan schedule with covenant definitions per facility Core DSCR and debt yield are defined differently in different loan documents; the definitions matter more than the ratios.
  • Most recent covenant compliance certificates Core As submitted, with the supporting calculation.
  • Affiliated and related-party fees charged to deals Core Asset management, property management, construction management, acquisition and disposition.
  • Development budgets, draw schedules and cost to complete Original budget against current reforecast, per project.
  • Promote and carried interest calculations, and any clawback exposure Including what has already been distributed against it.
  • LIHTC, HUD or other programme compliance where applicable Compliance period status and any findings.

When this stops being interesting and becomes urgent

  • A refinancing or a loan maturity, where covenant definitions get read closely
  • An investor asking a question about their capital account that takes a week to answer
  • A fund audit, or the first audit the sponsor has had
  • A partner separation, where the waterfall becomes contested rather than academic
  • A promote crystallisation event
