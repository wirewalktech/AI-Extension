Aerospace and defence

Here, a compliance lapse is not a fine. It is a contract you may no longer bid.

In most sectors compliance is a cost sitting beside the work. In this one it is a condition of being allowed to do the work at all, which makes it an operating question rather than a legal one. A review reads contract economics, rate structure and the compliance evidence together, because they are the same system.

  • ITAR
  • DFARS

The terms above are the ones the review uses with you, because they are the ones you already use. Nothing is translated into a consultant's vocabulary and then translated back.

Where it concentrates here

7 losses that recur in an aerospace and defence manufacturer and are worth going looking for. Each is concentrated — a single agreement, a single queue, a single account nobody reviews — which is precisely why a sample of the operation tends to miss them and a full pass tends to find them. They are not findings from a named engagement; they are what a review of an aerospace and defence manufacturer is built to look for.

  1. A NIST SP 800-171 self-assessment score submitted to SPRS with a plan of action behind it that was written to close the gap on paper rather than in the network.
  2. ITAR-controlled technical data on a shared drive that is segregated in the policy and reachable in practice.
  3. Indirect rate structure built around a programme that ended, with the overhead still allocated across work that cannot carry it.
  4. Build-to-print parts quoted from standards set several revisions ago, so reported margin by part number is a well-formatted guess.
  5. Long-lead material bought against a forecast that moved, sitting as inventory no programme will absorb.
  6. Scrap and rework booked to a variance account reviewed in total, so one bad process hides inside an acceptable aggregate.
  7. Cost of quality — containment, sorting, expedited freight after a slip — absorbed into overhead rather than attributed to the part that caused it.

Not every aerospace and defence manufacturer is the same business

  • Build-to-print machining and fabricationQuoted from standards that drift, on parts whose revisions change without the price being revisited.
  • Components and assembliesLong-lead material bought against forecasts that move, leaving inventory nobody will own.
  • MRO and aftermarketHighest margin, hardest to cost, and priced by precedent rather than by work actually performed.
  • Defence primes and subcontractorsCAS-covered contracts, incurred cost submissions, and a rate structure that must survive a DCAA audit.

Where this data actually lives

  • Epicor Kinetic, Global Shop or JobBOSS in the mid-market; IFS at the top end
  • A separate quality system — ETQ, uniPoint or paper — holding the AS9100 record
  • Net-Inspect or Discus for FAI and PPAP submissions
  • An ITAR-segregated file share that is usually less segregated than believed
  • Exostar or SAP Ariba for prime customer portals

Named because it changes what the intake asks for. An extract from one of these has known gaps, and the review is built around them rather than surprised by them.

The obligations that set the deadlines

  • AS9100D certification and surveillance audits
  • ITAR registration and export licensing; EAR classification for dual-use items
  • DFARS 252.204-7012 and NIST SP 800-171 — the SPRS score, and whether the POA&M behind it is real
  • CMMC assessment, and what it will find in the shared drive
  • DCAA incurred cost submissions and provisional billing rates on cost-type work
  • First Article Inspection (AS9102) and PPAP records

What the review would ask you for

9 further requests, specific to an aerospace and defence manufacturer — 5 of them core — on top of the 107 the intake asks of every organisation, across finance, revenue, receivables, payables, contracts, people, IT, operations, risk, commercial and governance.
  • Contract register: type, prime, period and value Core Firm-fixed-price, cost-plus and T&M carry entirely different exposure.
  • Indirect rate structure and provisional billing rates Core Overhead, G&A and any fringe pools, with the basis for each.
  • AS9100 certificate, last audit report and open findings Core Including surveillance audit dates.
  • NIST SP 800-171 assessment, SPRS score and POA&M Core The score as submitted, and the evidence behind the controls claimed met.
  • ITAR registration, export licences and technical data controls Core Including how ITAR-controlled data is segregated in practice, not in policy.
  • Scrap, rework and cost of poor quality by part number By part, not in total — a single bad process hides inside an acceptable aggregate.
  • On-time delivery and supplier scorecards from primes As the customer measures it, which is rarely as you measure it.
  • Inventory by programme, with obsolescence and long-lead exposure Material bought against a forecast that moved.
  • Most recent DCAA correspondence, incurred cost submissions Including any questioned costs and their resolution.

When this stops being interesting and becomes urgent

  • A CMMC assessment date, which converts a security question into a revenue question
  • A prime's supplier scorecard slipping
  • A DCAA audit or a questioned cost
  • An acquisition approach, where ITAR and CUI handling become diligence items
  • A programme ending and the overhead built around it staying
