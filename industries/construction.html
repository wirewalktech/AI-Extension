Construction and contracting

Built, and never billed.

Projects lose money in the gap between what was instructed and what was agreed. Variations get built because the programme demands it, and the paperwork follows later — or does not.

Where it concentrates here

Five losses that recur in construction and contracting and are worth going looking for. Each is concentrated — a single agreement, a single queue, a single account nobody reviews — which is precisely why a sample of the operation tends to miss them and a full pass tends to find them. They are not findings from a named engagement; they are what a review of construction and contracting is built to look for.

  1. Variations instructed verbally, built, and never converted into an approved change order, so the cost sits in the project and the revenue never arrives.
  2. Retention released late or not chased at all, ageing past the point anyone remembers the contract.
  3. Back-charges to subcontractors identified on site and never actually applied.
  4. Over- and under-billing on WIP that makes project margin look stable until the final account.
  5. Extension of time entitlements not claimed within the contractual notice period, so a valid claim expires unused.

Not every construction and contracting is the same business

  • General contractingMargin lives in the difference between the estimate, the buyout and the final cost, and the estimate is rarely revisited once the job is won.
  • Mechanical, electrical and specialty tradesChange orders performed before they are approved, then negotiated from a weak position because the work is already installed.
  • Heavy civil and public workCertified payroll, prevailing wage and DBE participation, where a compliance failure is withheld payment rather than a fine.
  • Service and maintenance divisionsThe steadiest margin in the business, usually run on the estimating assumptions of the construction side, which do not fit it.

Where this data actually lives

  • Sage 300 CRE (Timberline) or Viewpoint Vista in established contractors
  • Procore for project management, often not reconciled to the accounting system
  • Foundation or Computer Ease in specialty trades
  • A separate certified payroll process for any public work
  • Textura or GCPay for subcontractor payment and lien waiver tracking

Named because it changes what the intake asks for. An extract from one of these has known gaps, and the review is built around them rather than surprised by them.

The obligations that set the deadlines

  • Certified payroll (WH-347) and prevailing wage determinations on public work
  • Lien waivers, notices to owner and the statutory deadlines that void a claim
  • Surety bonding — the WIP schedule the bonding agent actually reads
  • OSHA 300 logs and EMR, which price the insurance
  • AIA G702/G703 applications and stored-materials substantiation

When this stops being interesting and becomes urgent

  • A bonding capacity review, where the WIP schedule is read by someone who knows how to read one
  • A job that lost money and nobody can say at which point it turned
  • An owner or generational transition
  • A bank line renewal against work-in-progress
  • An audit of a public contract

Who this involves

  • Signs it: Owner or president; CFO on anything touching the bonding relationship
  • Resists it: Project managers whose job margins become individually visible
  • Has been asking for it: The controller, and the bonding agent
